1. Anthropic says it cut model copying by 90–99%, and names five Chinese labs

Reasoning tokens harvested fell 10 to 100+ times once its classifier was fully in place, Anthropic says, with Alibaba's Qwen campaign at about 151 million exchanges. New API accounts can no longer edit prior context. The figures are Anthropic's own and unverified. (Anthropic · AI Weekly, 2026-10-12)

Analyst view. Model theft by query is now a named operational threat with a defence budget, and it changes what a model API is: a metered service whose provider inspects usage patterns and may block or bill you for traffic it dislikes. The figures are Anthropic's own and no third party has verified the 90–99% claim. For enterprises the practical effects are false positives and changing terms, plus a reminder that open-weights models from other labs may carry capabilities copied from closed ones. Confidence: high that the defences exist and are deployed; low on the effectiveness figures until independently tested.

2. Amex wants AI agents to carry a signed proof of what you asked them to buy

The Intent Intelligence Framework records customer limits, issues a Proof of Intent Token and lets the agent pay with a digital credential, not the card number. Amex has not yet seen more agentic disputes. (TechCrunch, 2026-10-13)

Analyst view. This is the payments industry turning "human in the loop" into a data object. A signed, user-approved intent that travels with the transaction is the same pattern supervisors are asking banks to show for agents (covered 13 October), and it gives merchants and issuers a basis for deciding who is liable when an agent strays. The open question is adoption: a standard that only one network supports does not help a merchant. Confidence: high on what was announced; medium on whether it becomes a standard.

Deep Dive: The Control Layer Is the Product — intent, inventory and provenance become what enterprises buy

Five stories from Monday and Tuesday look unrelated, and are the same story. Anthropic is building a classifier to decide whose traffic counts as abuse. Amex is putting a signed record of intent between an agent and a card. Pluto sells an inventory of the AI tools and agents that employees brought in without asking. A bankrupt chatbot business shows what happens when licence, liability and revenue sit in different hands. Apple's case shows that even hiring records are now evidence. In each, the value has moved from the model to the layer around it.

Intent is becoming a data object. Amex's Proof of Intent Token records what the customer approved and when. That is the payments version of what bank supervisors asked for last week (covered 13 October): limits, approvals and an audit trail. A firm that can show, for each agent action, which human limit authorised it will move faster through approvals than one that logs only the action.

Inventory comes before governance. Pluto's central claim is that tools appear in a company within two hours of release and that most risk sits outside engineering. Whatever the exact share, a policy cannot cover what the company does not know it runs. The inventory has to include employee-built apps, extensions and MCP servers.

Providers are policing their own APIs. Anthropic's defences show that access to a frontier model now comes with surveillance of usage patterns, throttling and billing for blocked traffic. That is reasonable for the provider, and it means a customer's agent loops, replays and bulk jobs can look like abuse. Contract terms and appeal routes matter more than before.

Suppliers can fail. Character.AI's filing is a reminder to price vendor risk: a model licence sold to a hyperscaler in 2024, a safety liability left behind, and a buyer who wants only part of the assets. Exit plans for AI vendors are a procurement control, not a legal formality.
